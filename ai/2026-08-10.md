> **Ninth Circuit ruling in Amazon v. Perplexity redefines who "accesses" a website when an agent acts for a user** — *US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, opinion filed 2026-08-04; enterprise-legal analysis cresting through the weekend*
> The Ninth Circuit vacated Amazon's preliminary injunction against Perplexity's Comet browser tool, holding that when a user tasks a Perplexity agent with taking actions on the user's behalf on Amazon.com, it is "the user who 'accessed' Amazon's computers," not Perplexity — and that "access" under the CFAA and California's CDAFA means access by a person, not a software tool. The case is remanded for further proceedings on contract and tort theories, which remain open. Why it matters: this is now the reference case for agent-vs-website access rights, and it materially reshapes the risk model for two groups. **First, agentic-browsing vendors** (Perplexity Comet, OpenAI Operator, Anthropic Computer Use, Meta Muse Spark's browser tool, Google's Antigravity CLI) — CFAA and CDAFA theories are removed as a bar-and-block instrument; enforcement will now route through contract, tort, or trademark. **Second, e-commerce and content publishers** relying on CFAA theories to block agents — their doctrinal cover has just narrowed to contract-based Terms of Service enforcement (which the panel notably declined to weaken but did not reach). Contract-based blocking is meaningful but slower, more per-vendor, and harder to enforce at scale than a CFAA injunction. Cooley's Aug 6 note reads the opinion as a leading-edge doctrinal move that other circuits are likely to test. **EU/Nordic angle:** indirect but real — EU e-commerce and content players evaluating anti-bot / anti-agent posture cannot rely on US CFAA-equivalent enforcement; the practical operational posture across US and EU is now more aligned than it was two weeks ago. ## Counter-narrative

The day's biggest single-vendor story remains the Astra Preparedness pause, but on a slow Sunday the actual dominant story is the *collective silence* from Anthropic, Google, Nvidia, and the EU AI Office. The skeptical case on the silence:

- **The silence may not be signalling anything at all.** August 10 is a Sunday in the middle of the European and US summer holiday window. Anthropic's RSP updates historically require multiple weeks of internal drafting, review, and legal sign-off; a 6-day gap after a competitor disclosure is well inside normal. Google's Frontier Safety Framework and Nvidia's investor-communications discipline both cluster to specific calendar windows (product launches, earnings-adjacent), not reactive same-week responses. Reading the silence as "everyone is stalling" may be a market-narrative construct rather than a real-world one — and treating the silence as a scoring input for procurement decisions could produce worse decisions than waiting one more week.
- **The counter-counter:** even granting the summer / drafting-cycle explanation, the market is going to trade on the framing whether or not the framing is accurate. So the operational advice — "expect a matching disclosure inside 5–7 business days, and note if it doesn't happen" — holds. Both things can be true: the silence is probably not signalling anything material, and the market will price it as if it is.

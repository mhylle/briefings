## Counter-narrative

Skeptical case on the day's biggest story (CNBC's "model fatigue" survey):

- **"Fatigue" is a framing choice that flatters buyers and lets vendors continue.** A 62% "overwhelmed" number is not evidence of a broken cadence; it is evidence that a specific class of decision (side-by-side model comparison across six frontier releases in 96 hours) is being asked of a role (AI lead at a 500+ FTE enterprise) that in most cases lacks the bandwidth to answer it. The correct engineering answer is not "labs should slow down" — the labs are optimising for capability compounding on their own release cadence — but "buyers should stop trying to compare frontier releases individually and adopt a router-plus-abstraction pattern that makes the comparison automatic." The CNBC framing implicitly asks the vendors to slow; that is the ask least likely to be met and the ask that would help buyers least. Enterprise readers should not use this story as evidence to lobby vendors; they should use it as evidence to fund internal routing/abstraction work.
- **Altman's on-record acknowledgment is a positioning move as much as a comment.** OpenAI shipped the roughest launch of the week (staged Daybreak-first Astra rollout, public apology 4 Sept, Pro-access commitment still on the line today); Altman naming the industry-wide cadence problem shifts the story from an OpenAI operational-execution problem to a whole-market pace problem. The single-vendor read has become a category read; that is a good outcome for the vendor with the most visible operational miss of the wave.
- **The survey design is not published.** No methodology (sampling frame, response rate, question wording, weightings) is in the CNBC piece as reproduced through the indexed secondary reports available in this environment. 62% is a headline number; the confidence interval is unknown. Enterprise procurement teams should cite the story as a pattern, not the number as a fact, until the underlying methodology is published.

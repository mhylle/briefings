## Headlines

> **Dragos canary-token study (HD Moore, Chief Research Officer): at least three AI providers exposed to active credential exfiltration; ~2 M bait prompts July → November 2026; AWS canary keys actively re-used by attackers; some US-submitted prompts forwarded to Chinese AI organizations** — *Dragos, surfaced in-window Sat 2026-11-21 (primary publish timestamp not reachable in-session; Dragos host blocked)*
> HD Moore's research team at Dragos ran a five-month study (July → November 2026) that submitted approximately 2 M prompts containing unique URLs and canary-token credentials — including AWS keys seeded in controlled test accounts — to widely used AI services, then monitored canary-activation telemetry for downstream use. The post, titled "Three breaches, endless leaks: Your AI supply chain needs canaries," reports: activity indicating data from at least three AI providers was exposed; attackers actively using the seeded test prompts to access the controlled test systems and AWS canary accounts; earliest incident dated mid-July 2026; the largest observed breach involved "a couple hundred" AWS keys; and prompts submitted to US-based providers in some cases being transmitted to Chinese AI organizations. None of the three implicated providers were aware of the breach prior to Dragos's notification. Specific provider names are not disclosed in reachable excerpt. Primary Dragos blog host (`www.dragos.com`) is blocked in this session; the study's parameters, findings, and author attribution are consistent across multiple secondary pickups including Nov-21 AI-Weekly and Sam-on-AI daily indices, Nov-21 Ars Technica (per secondary roundup citation; direct Ars reachability not verified in-session), and search-engine preview excerpts of the Dragos blog itself.
> Why it matters: this is the first systematic multi-provider empirical demonstration of AI-supply-chain data exfiltration published by a named security vendor with a reproducible methodology — prior work (Wiz 90-day honeypot study, Bitsight, Darktrace agentic-harness hijack papers) measured infrastructure-level attacks, not inter-provider credential propagation. The operational implication for enterprise AI architecture is specific: credentials, API keys, access tokens, and bearer-secrets passed through prompts to third-party AI services cannot be assumed to remain in-provider. The reported activity confirms that where a provider is breached, seeded credentials are actively re-used by attackers; and that in some provider topologies, US-submitted prompts leak to jurisdictions and organizations not disclosed to the submitter. For enterprises with sensitive data in prompts: prompt-content governance, secrets-scrubbing at the gateway, and provider supply-chain due diligence are now backed by field evidence, not threat-modeling speculation. For OpenAI and other providers whose Nov-20 Usage Policies tightened the intermediary-approval regime: the Dragos findings add a separate pressure vector on intermediary platforms — not just the contractual-approval test, but a security-posture test that routers, gateways, and inference resellers will be asked by enterprise customers to attest to. **For EU / Nordic readers:** the "US-submitted prompts transmitted to Chinese AI organizations" finding sits squarely inside GDPR Article 44–50 transfer-restriction framing and EU AI Act Article 26 "deployer" obligations — any EU enterprise routing prompts through a provider where cross-border onward transfer to a third-country AI organization is possible now has empirical evidence to cite in a DPIA. For Nordic public-sector procurement and defense end-use: this is a material procurement-security input for 2027 contracts. ## Counter-narrative

- **The Dragos finding may be sensationalized at the "AI supply-chain" framing level — three implicated providers out of the ~N tested is a credential-exfiltration rate, not a systemic-AI-supply-chain-compromise rate.** A skeptical read: canary-token studies in adjacent domains (cloud-provider credential leakage, SaaS-connector supply chain) regularly find 2–5 providers compromised in any given quarter; the Dragos finding may be consistent with baseline cloud-ecosystem credential-leakage rates rather than AI-specific. The study's framing emphasizes "AI supply chain" but the exploited vector — AWS canary keys seeded in test prompts — is a known-plaintext credential-handling pattern that would be compromised in any environment where provider-side logging or data-handling is non-conformant. Enterprise procurement teams should treat the Dragos findings as an input to supply-chain diligence, not as an industry-wide indictment. The three providers are not named; until named, the headline risk-posture adjustment is cautionary, not definitive. On the other hand, the "prompts to US providers transmitted to Chinese AI organizations" finding is the more structurally novel claim and is where a procurement-grade follow-up should concentrate — that is not a baseline-credential-leakage pattern, that is a jurisdictional-flow pattern specific to inter-model data sharing. If primary Dragos-published provider names emerge in the Mon Nov-23 → Fri Nov-27 window, procurement posture may need a more directive adjustment.
